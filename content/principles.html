09 / First principles

Think beyond the next land boom.

The best lesson from a missed land purchase is about how to make decisions under uncertainty. It is not evidence that buying whatever looks unattractive today will work.

The strongest case against the main recommendation

Software and technical-adjacent work may be crowded; employers may expect more from beginners as AI tools improve, and a practical skill gap may take longer than six months to close. A route that builds valuable skills can still miss your immediate income deadline.

Stanford’s August 2026 update reports a roughly 19% relative employment shortfall for US workers aged 22–25 in highly AI-exposed roles compared with less-exposed peers, mainly through hiring. The authors describe this as observational, not causal, and discuss sample and education-related caveats. It is a reason to take entry risk seriously—not a Bangladesh forecast.[26]

Why still test the route: some readers already have access to technical study and computing tools, and the skills can transfer. For those readers, that can justify a bounded experiment. It does not justify betting all your runway on a guaranteed tech outcome.

Why the past looks easier than it was

Today you know which city expanded, which road was built, and which parcel became valuable. At the time, buyers faced uncertain infrastructure, possible title disputes, illiquidity, carrying costs, and competing family needs. Some unattractive land stayed unattractive. Some owners could not hold long enough to benefit. A fair comparison includes those outcomes.

Without knowing the prices, alternatives, obligations and information available to your family then, we cannot conclude that they made a bad decision. Their spending may also have created something valuable: education, health, safety, or the time you now have to build a career.

Judge a decision by the evidence and risks available when you made it. Judge the outcome separately.

The economic chain that must actually work

Growing need → a buyer with a budget → a solution you can deliver → a reason to choose you → enough retained income → reinvestment over time

Every arrow can fail. Society may need elder care, but households may not afford professional care. AI may create value, while competition pushes down the price of generic AI services. A booming sector may already command expensive stock valuations. Being early is only useful if you can survive until demand arrives.

For a career

Your earning power depends on useful competence, the scarcity of reliable alternatives, access to employers or buyers, and your bargaining position. A credential can open a door; proof of work helps you stay in the room.

For a business

Revenue is price × paying customers. Owner earnings subtract delivery, selling, support, payment losses, tax and the cost of your own time. A popular product can still be an exhausting low-margin business.

For an investment

Your return depends on what you pay, future cash flows or resale value, costs, inflation and risk. A great industry can be a poor investment at an excessive price.

For an early-career starting point

Common constraints include delivery ability and access to paying demand. Buying more hardware or searching for speculative assets does not remove either constraint.

Look for neglected work, not merely unpopular work

Unpopular opportunities come in different forms. Some are unpleasant but necessary: cleaning data, debugging failures, documentation, collections and maintenance. Others are unpopular for good reasons: no buyer budget, poor economics, unsafe conditions, legal problems, or obsolete products. Test which kind you have found.

What the research does—and does not—say about AI

EXPOSURE

The ILO finds substantial task exposure and expects job transformation to be more common than total replacement. It does not say that exposed work has no future, or that unexposed work is well paid.[01]

OBSERVATION

A published customer-support study found an average 15% productivity gain with AI assistance, with different effects across workers. That is evidence from a particular workplace, not a universal salary or productivity forecast.[03]

COUNTERPOINT

METR’s early-2025 experiment found experienced developers took longer with AI. Its February 2026 update warns that later estimates are affected by selection bias and suggests the picture has changed. Neither result justifies a blanket “AI helps everyone” or “AI is useless” conclusion.[04]

My inference: use AI, but build the ability to specify, inspect, test and repair its work. Measure time to a correct, accepted result. A fast first draft that creates hidden defects is not a business advantage.

Inherited assets, geography, health and family obligations affect outcomes. A better process improves your choices; it cannot make structural constraints or luck disappear. Avoid blaming yourself—or your family—for every counterfactual fortune.
